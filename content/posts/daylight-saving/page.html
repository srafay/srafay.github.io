    Felt tired, flat or a little foggy since the start of October? It might not be you. It might be the clock.

    At 2 am on Sunday 4 October 2026, an hour went missing across south-east Australia.1 Most of us slept through it. We get it back on 4 April 2027, without interest.1

    Nobody asked us.

    That is not quite true everywhere. Western Australia has voted on daylight saving four times. Queensland voted in 1992.3 NSW voted once, in 1976.2 So anyone in NSW younger than about 68 has never had a say. In Victoria, South Australia, Tasmania and the ACT, I could not find a vote at all.3

    I am not here to tell you that daylight saving is good or bad. The evidence goes both ways, and this post lays it out. Lighter evenings come with fewer robberies and safer walks home.4 The first days after the spring change come with more cardiac arrests.5 Reasonable people weigh those differently.

    That is exactly why it should be a vote.

    The idea

    At each state election, add one question to the ballot paper. Daylight saving for the next term: yes or no? Majority wins, and the answer holds until the next election. Then we ask again.

    It is not a new idea. NSW held its 1976 vote on the same day as the state election.2 Western Australia passed a three-year trial in 2006, with a referendum built in at the end.3

    What is in this post

    Ten short sections follow. They cover what the spring change does to our bodies, and how a clerk who collected insects started all this. They cover the votes, the power bills, the case for lighter evenings and the case for darker ones. There are a few cows, and one roadhouse sign that says it best.

    If you already agree, sign the petition for a vote. Section 10 also has a short message you can send to your state MP. This post does not ask to end daylight saving. It asks for a say. If you want the detail first, read on.

    And if you have felt off since October, tell the people you live with. Ask how they slept. You may not be the only one.

    One lost hour

    On Sunday 4 October 2026, at 2 am, the clocks in south-east Australia jumped to 3 am.13 It happened at the hour when most of us were asleep and could not object.

    The Monday was harder. In Melbourne, the sun rose at 6 am the Monday before. On Monday 5 October, it rose at 6:49.13 The alarm rang at the usual time on the clock. To the body, 6:30 felt like 5:30.

    That lost hour is real. In the UK, 11,780 people wore wrist monitors around the clock changes.9 On the spring Sunday, they slept 65 minutes less than the Sunday before.9 The autumn change gave back only 33 minutes.9 So we lose a full hour in spring and win back about half of it in autumn. Not a great exchange rate.

    The clock change affects me every year. So I went looking for what it does to everyone else.

    What our ambulances saw

    Ambulance Victoria keeps a record of every cardiac arrest its crews attend. A Monash University team used it to study the clock changes.1 They looked at 89,409 adult cardiac arrests outside hospital, from 2000 to 2020.1

    On the Sunday the clocks went forward, the risk rose 13 per cent.1 Over that Sunday and the Monday after, it ran 17 per cent above normal.1

    Then came the surprise. The autumn change worked the other way. By the end of the week after we gain an hour, the risk was 30 per cent lower than usual.1

    More heart attacks, or earlier ones?

    The best-known study comes from Michigan. Researchers counted heart attacks that needed a procedure to reopen a blocked artery, from 2010 to 2013.2 The hospitals in the study treat about 32 of these on a normal Monday. On the Monday after the spring change, they treated about eight more.3 That is 24 per cent more than expected.2

    Here is the twist. Across the whole week, the total did not change.2 The Monday rush tapered off over the days that followed.3 The heart attacks were not extra. They were early, like guests who turn up an hour before the party.2

    Sweden found something similar. Heart attacks rose about 5 per cent in the first week after the spring change, mostly in the first three days.5 Then, in 2025, a US study of 168,870 patients at 1,124 hospitals found no rise at all.4 So the heart attack question is still open.

    On the roads

    In the United States, a study of 732,835 fatal crashes found a 6 per cent rise after the spring change.6 The rise was largest in the morning, and it faded within the week.6 The autumn change made no difference.6

    Australia tells a different story. Jonathan James of the University of Bath studied more than 40,000 fatal crashes here, from 1989 to 2015.8 He found no overall change.8 The crashes moved instead. There were fewer in the evening and more earlier in the day.8 Hold on to that evening result. It comes back in section 5.

    At work, and in the brain

    US miners had more injuries, and worse ones, on the Monday after the spring change.10 Workers slept about 40 minutes less that night.10 The autumn change made no difference.10

    In Finland, stroke admissions were 8 per cent higher in the first two days after a clock change.11 Across the whole week, the rise was too small to be sure of.11

    The weeks after

    One more Australian study is worth knowing about. Michael Berk and colleagues looked at Australian suicide data from 1971 to 2001.12 Male suicides rose in the weeks after daylight saving began.12 If this brings anything up for you, Lifeline is on 13 11 14.

    What it adds up to

    The spring change costs us sleep, and the first two days carry real risk. Most of it is gone within a week. Some of it only moves harm from one day to another.

    If you have felt tired or a little off since 4 October, this may be part of the reason. You lost about an hour of sleep, and your body clock is still catching up.9 Tell the people you live with. Ask how they slept. You may find you are not the only one.

    Is that worth an extra hour of evening light? Some people will say yes without blinking. Others will say no just as fast. Both are fair answers, and both deserve to be counted.

    So why did anyone start moving the clocks in the first place? The story begins in Paris in 1784, with a joke about candles.

    Part 2: How it started

    Ask who invented daylight saving, and many people will say Benjamin Franklin. The real answer is a man in Wellington who wanted more time to catch insects.35

    The clerk and his insects

    George Vernon Hudson joined the Wellington post office as a cadet in 1883.3 The job ran on shifts. That left him daylight hours free to collect insects, study their life cycles and paint them.3 Over his life he published more than 2,500 coloured insect paintings.3

    Shift work taught him what daylight was worth. On 16 October 1895, he gave a paper to the Wellington Philosophical Society.3 He wanted the clocks moved two hours forward in summer.5 One member called the idea "wholly unscientific and impracticable".4 Others laughed at it.3

    Hudson had the last laugh, more or less. New Zealand trialled one hour of summer time in 1927.3

    So where does Franklin come in?

    On 26 April 1784, the Journal de Paris printed a letter signed "A Subscriber". Franklin wrote it.1

    In the letter, a noise wakes him at six in the morning. His room is full of light, because his servant forgot to close the shutters.2 He is amazed to learn that the sun rises so early. Then he works out how many candles Paris burns while it sleeps through the morning.2

    His fixes were absurd on purpose. Tax every window with shutters. Ration each family to one pound of candles a week.2 Ring every church bell at sunrise, and if that fails, "let cannon be fired in every street".2 The cannon idea never caught on.

    He never suggested moving the clocks.2 But one line sounds familiar after section 1: "All the difficulty will be in the first two or three days".2

    The builder on a morning ride

    In Britain, William Willett had the idea on his own. The story goes that he was riding near his home early one summer morning. He noticed how many blinds were still down.6

    In July 1907, he paid to print a pamphlet, The Waste of Daylight.6 He wanted the clocks moved forward 80 minutes, 20 minutes at a time, on four Sundays in April.7 They would come back the same way in September.7 He reckoned it would save £2.5 million in lighting.6

    An MP took up his bill. A young Winston Churchill backed it for a time. Parliament still did nothing.6 Willett died of influenza in March 1915.6 A sundial in his memory stands in Petts Wood, near his home. It is set permanently to daylight saving time. It is the one clock in England that never has to change.6

    The war that made it law

    Then coal ran short. Germany and Austria-Hungary moved their clocks on 30 April 1916, to save it.8 Britain followed on Sunday 21 May.6

    Tasmania went first in Australia, on 1 October 1916.814 A federal law put every state on daylight saving from 1 January 1917.14 It lasted one summer. The law was repealed in September 1917.14 Tasmania kept going on its own for two more summers, until 1919.14

    The Second World War brought it back. Rules signed on Christmas Eve 1941 moved the clocks forward on New Year's Day 1942.12 Daylight saving ran for three wartime summers. The last ended in March 1944.1114

    A drought in Tasmania

    For more than 20 years after that, the clocks stayed put. Then came 1967. A drought left Tasmania's hydro-electric dams low, and power rationing was a real risk.10 Tasmania brought daylight saving back that October to save power.1014 It has used it every summer since.8

    In 1971, NSW, Victoria, South Australia, the ACT and Queensland followed.8 NSW moved its clocks on 31 October 1971.11 Western Australia and the Northern Territory stayed out.8 Since 2008, the states that use it have shared the same dates.8 It runs from the first Sunday in October to the first Sunday in April.13

    Who decided

    Look back over those dates and notice who made each call. A wartime government in 1916. A federal parliament in 1917. A Tasmanian government in a drought in 1967. State governments in 1971.814 Each had its reasons. None of them put the question to a public vote first.

    Queensland lasted a single summer and dropped it in 1972.8 That was not the end of the argument. It was the start of a fight that went to the ballot box again and again. Along the way it gave us a famous line about cows that nobody can trace.

    Daylight saving will fade your curtains. It will confuse the cows. These are the arguments everyone remembers.10

    They are often pinned on Queensland Premier Joh Bjelke-Petersen. I could not find a record of him saying either one. By 1997 the jokes were old enough that one reviewer complained about them. She called them "fables about fading curtains and crazed farm animals".14

    The cows, for the record, have never been asked either. People have, in some states. Those votes tell a better story than the cows.

    NSW: asked once, in 1976

    NSW started daylight saving in 1971. Five summers later, it asked. On 1 May 1976, voters at the state election also answered a question on daylight saving.913 Yes won 1,882,770 votes to 868,900.9 That is about 68 per cent.

    It was cheap and simple. The question rode along with an election people were already voting in. NSW has kept daylight saving ever since, and has never asked again.

    Western Australia: four tries, four noes

    Western Australia has run four trials. Each ended in a referendum, and each referendum said no.2

    The first trial ran over the summer of 1974 to 1975. On 8 March 1975, No won 53.7 per cent.3 The second came in 1983 to 1984, and No won 54.4 per cent.4 The third was the shortest, from November 1991 to March 1992. No won again, with 53.1 per cent, the closest of the four.5

    The fourth is the most interesting. In 2006, Parliament passed a three-year trial with a referendum built in at the end.16 People got to live with it first, then decide. On 16 May 2009, No won 54.6 per cent.6 Business groups backed Yes, to narrow the summer gap with the eastern states.6 The No vote was strongest in the country.6

    Whatever you think of the answer, Western Australians got to give it. Four times.

    Queensland: asked once, then told no

    Queensland joined in 1971 and dropped daylight saving after one summer.2 It tried again from 1989, for three summers.1 The 1989 cabinet papers show one worry nobody jokes about. Vince Lester, the industrial affairs minister, said many people in the bush were concerned about their TV programs.12

    Then the state asked voters. On 22 February 1992, No won with 54.5 per cent.1 Almost 90 per cent of voters turned out.1

    The total hides a split. In the south-east seat of Albert, 65 per cent voted yes.11 In Burdekin, in north Queensland, 17 per cent did.11

    Since then, Queenslanders have kept asking to be asked. A 2006 petition drew 62,232 signatures.16 Research commissioned by the government found 59 per cent of Queenslanders in favour in 2007, and 69 per cent in the south-east.16 The new Premier, Anna Bligh, ruled out another referendum anyway.16 Section 7 picks up the story from there.

    Everyone else

    Victoria, South Australia, Tasmania and the ACT have used daylight saving for more than 50 years. I could not find a single statewide vote on it in any of them.2 It started by decision of government, and it has stayed that way.

    Elsewhere

    The United States has fought daylight saving more than once. In 1919, Congress ended it nationally, over President Woodrow Wilson's veto.815 Farmers had complained that it cost them a morning hour to get milk to market.8

    In January 1974, during an oil crisis, the US went further. It put the clocks forward and left them there all year.15 Parents worried about children leaving for school before sunrise.8 Florida's governor asked Congress to repeal the law.8 From 27 October 1974, the country went back to standard time for winter.15 The experiment did not survive its first winter.

    Russia tried the same thing in 2011. It moved its clocks forward and never moved them back.7 After three winters, most of the country moved back an hour in October 2014, and stayed there.7

    What the votes say

    Where daylight saving already ran every summer, as in NSW, people voted to keep it. Where it came as a trial, as in Queensland and Western Australia, they voted it out. In those five No votes, the margin was never more than ten points.

    So the votes are close, and they go both ways. That is the strongest reason to keep holding them.

    Every one of these fights also began with the same promise: coal in 1916, water in Tasmania in 1967. Daylight saving would save energy. So does it? In 2000, the Sydney Olympics gave two economists a way to find out.

    Part 4: Does it save energy?

    About zeroThe energy promise

    Victoria tested the power saving in 2000. The change was too small to tell from nothing.

    Victoria, with South Australia for comparison. 2000.

    In short

    Daylight saving was sold on saving coal. Does it save power today? In 2000, Victoria started it two months early for the Sydney Olympics. Evening demand fell, morning demand rose, and the net change was about zero.1 In Indiana, households used about 1 per cent more.2 Across 44 studies, the average saving is about a third of 1 per cent a day.4 The original reason has mostly gone. That is a good moment to ask people again.

    In 1916 the promise was coal. In 1967 it was the water in Tasmania's dams. You will still hear that daylight saving saves power. In 2000, Australia tested that claim without meaning to.

    The Olympic experiment

    For the Sydney Olympics, Victoria and NSW started daylight saving two months early.1 Two economists, Ryan Kellogg and Hendrik Wolff, saw their chance. Victoria hosted no Olympic events, so the Games did not distort its power use.1 South Australia kept its usual start date, so it could serve as the comparison.1

    The evening went to plan. Demand fell between 5 pm and 7:30 pm, when people needed less lighting and heating.1

    The morning did not. Demand rose between 7 am and 8 am.1 People still got up at the same time on the clock. Now they woke before sunrise and switched the lights on.1

    Add the two together and you get almost nothing. Over the whole extension, demand rose 0.09 per cent, too small to tell apart from zero.1 In September alone, it rose 0.39 per cent.1 A California model had predicted a 0.6 per cent saving. The Victorian data ruled that out.1

    Indiana

    Indiana is a rare place where you can compare before and after. Until 2006, most of the state never moved its clocks.5 Then, on 2 April 2006, all of it did.5

    Matthew Kotchen and Laura Grant had more than 7 million monthly power bills from southern Indiana, across three years.2 After the change, household electricity use went up, not down, by about 1 per cent.2 In autumn, the rise reached 2 to 4 per cent.2 Less lighting was needed, but more heating and cooling made up for it.2 They put the cost to Indiana households at $9 million a year in extra bills.2

    The US extension

    In 2007, the United States added four weeks to daylight saving. It hoped to cut electricity use by 1 per cent.1 The Department of Energy later measured a saving of 0.5 per cent on each day of the extension.3 Across the whole year, that came to 0.03 per cent.3 That is three hundredths of one per cent. You would struggle to find it on a power bill.

    All the studies together

    What happens if you pool everything? Tomas Havranek, Dominik Herman and Zuzana Irsova combined 162 estimates from 44 studies.4 The average was a saving of 0.34 per cent on days when daylight saving applies.4

    Where you live matters. The saving was larger far from the equator.4 Subtropical places used more electricity because of daylight saving, not less.4 Brisbane is subtropical. Hold that thought for section 7.

    Western Australia

    Western Australia's trial from 2006 to 2009 gave a local test. Seungmoon Choi, Alistair Pellen and Virginie Masson found little change in total demand.6 Demand moved instead, away from the late afternoon and early evening.6

    What it adds up to

    Daylight saving moves electricity use from the evening to the morning.16 Whether it saves anything depends on where you live and the time of year.4 Air conditioning has changed the daily pattern since the wartime idea of saving on lighting.1

    So the reason it was sold on has mostly faded. That does not mean daylight saving should go. It means the original deal no longer explains why we keep it. When the reason for a rule changes, it is fair to ask people again.

    If energy is not the reason to keep it, what is? Supporters have a better answer than power bills. It involves robbers, and the hour after sunset.

    On Monday 28 September, the sun set over Sydney at 5:56 pm. On Monday 5 October, it set at 7:01.8 The walk home from the station went from dusk to broad daylight in a week.

    Most of the case against daylight saving is about the morning. The case for it is about that evening hour. So what changes when it is light?

    Robbers prefer the dark

    Jennifer Doleac and Nicholas Sanders asked that question about crime. The spring change gave them a neat test. Overnight, the hour around sunset goes from dark to light, and nothing else changes.1

    Robberies fell about 7 per cent in the weeks after the change.1 Most of the drop came from that one hour. Around sunset, robberies fell 27 per cent.1 You might expect the darker mornings to make up for it. They did not. The authors saw no change in morning crime.1 Robbers, it seems, are not early risers.

    This is one study, in one country. It still points one way.

    Safer streets for walkers

    A 2025 study looked at every fatal crash in the mainland United States from 2010 to 2019.2 In the five weeks after the spring change, pedestrian and cyclist deaths fell 24 per cent.2 In the five weeks after the autumn change, they rose 13 per cent.2 The authors tied almost all of it to the light at the time of the crash.2

    Drivers and passengers were a different story. Their fatal crashes rose 12 per cent after the spring change.2 Only a small part of that was linked to light.2 Section 1 has a likely culprit: an hour less sleep.

    Remember the Australian crash study from section 1? It found no change in fatal crashes overall.3 But under daylight saving, there were fewer crashes in the evening and more earlier in the day.3

    An older US model went further. It estimated that daylight saving all year would save 171 pedestrians a year.4 That was 13 per cent of pedestrian deaths in the morning and evening peaks.4

    Children at play

    Anna Goodman and colleagues pooled activity data from 23,188 children in nine countries.5 For 439 of them, the data covered the days just before and after a clock change.5 Longer evening light came with a small rise in activity, mostly in the late afternoon and early evening.5 It showed up in the European, English and two Australian samples.5

    The gain for each child is small. The authors argue that small gains across a whole population still add up.5

    Golf, tourism and public opinion

    The tourism argument is mostly opinion. In 2023, Brisbane Lord Mayor Adrian Schrinner said daylight saving "would deliver a bonanza for our local tourism and hospitality industries".7 Thomas Sigler of the University of Queensland notes that golf clubs are "some of the biggest proponents of daylight saving".6 Nobody has yet measured the cows' view of golf.

    Public opinion looks clear. In 2024, Sigler reported a representative survey of more than 1,100 Australians.6 It found 80 per cent support, with a majority even in Queensland and Western Australia.6 The office of Western Australian MP Wilson Tucker funded it.6

    A survey is not a vote, though. The article does not give the question wording.6 And 1,100 people is a sample, not a state. If support really is that high, a vote would settle it in daylight saving's favour. Supporters have nothing to lose by asking.

    Where the case is thin

    The crime and crash studies are American. The children's activity gain is small. The Australian crash data shows evening crashes moving, not vanishing. The evidence says lighter evenings make the evening safer, and the morning pays part of the bill.

    That morning bill is where sleep scientists come in. Most of them want the clock changes gone. Many want something that might surprise you: standard time all year, not daylight saving.

    There is a clock inside you that has never seen the one on your wall. It has never read a government gazette either.

    Light sets the clock

    Your body runs on a cycle of about 24 hours, the circadian rhythm.1 Left alone, it drifts. Light is the strongest cue that keeps it in time.7

    When the light arrives matters. Light in the morning moves the body clock earlier. Light in the evening pushes it later.34 The bright sky on your walk to the bus is doing a job. It tells your body the day has started.

    The sun does not move

    Daylight saving changes the wall clock, not the sun. One group of scientists describes it as moving a place one time zone further east.3 The body clock keeps following the sun, so the two drift apart.

    Melbourne shows the gap. On 11 October 2026, the sun was highest at 1:06 pm on the clock.10 On standard time, that moment would read 12:06 pm. Lunch, by the sun, is at one.

    Now picture daylight saving all year. In late June, Melbourne's sun rises at about 7:36 am.1013 On permanent daylight saving, that would be 8:36 am. In Hobart, it would be 8:42 am.1113 Many children would leave for school before sunrise, every winter school day.

    Social jet lag

    You have probably felt this one without a name for it. In 2006, Till Roenneberg's group called it "social jet lag".2 It is the gap between when your body wants to sleep and when work or school makes you get up. Night owls feel it most.2 A later study linked more social jet lag with a higher body mass index.12 That is a link, not proof of cause.

    Daylight saving widens that gap. Thomas Kantermann and colleagues surveyed 55,000 people in Central Europe.5 Under standard time, their sleep on days off moved with the seasonal shift of dawn. Under daylight saving, it did not.5

    A second group was tracked for eight weeks around each change.5 They adjusted easily in autumn. In spring, the night owls never fully adjusted.5

    What sleep scientists say

    The American Academy of Sleep Medicine took a position in 2020. It said the clock changes "should be abolished in favor of a fixed, national, year-round standard time".6 It noted that the long-term effects of permanent daylight saving "have not been well studied".6 In 2024 it went further, calling permanent standard time "the optimal choice for health and safety".7

    The Society for Research on Biological Rhythms agreed in 2019. Its experts concluded that "the advantages of permanent ST outweigh switching to DST annually or permanently".9

    In Australia

    The Sleep Health Foundation does not take a side on standard time or permanent daylight saving.1 It does warn that the spring change "can increase the risk of sleep loss and drowsiness".1 The body clock runs a little longer than 24 hours, so going to bed later is easier than going to bed earlier.1 That is why the April change feels easier than October. I could not find a position from the Australasian Sleep Association.

    Experts and voters

    So the sleep scientists want one thing: standard time, all year. The survey in section 5 suggests most Australians want to keep daylight saving.14 Those two can both be sincere.

    That is not a reason to ignore either side. It is a reason to put the evidence in front of people and let them choose. Experts should inform that choice. Voters should make it.

    If morning light matters this much, Queensland has a point. In December, the sun rises over Brisbane before 5 am.

    Say you set an alarm for 2:30 am on Sunday 4 October 2026, in Sydney. What should it have done?

    There was no right answer. At 2 am that morning, the clocks jumped straight to 3 am.13 For everyone in NSW, Victoria, the ACT, South Australia and Tasmania, 2:30 am did not exist.

    April has the opposite problem. On Sunday 4 April 2027, the clocks go back from 3 am to 2 am.12 The hour from 2 am to 3 am happens twice.

    People shrug this off. Computers cannot. They did not get a vote either, and they complain the loudest.

    The time that happens twice

    A program that stores "2:30 am, 4 April 2027, Sydney" has a problem. That time happens twice, an hour apart. Python, for example, keeps an extra flag called fold to say which of the two it means.13

    The safer habit is to avoid local time altogether. Coordinated Universal Time, UTC, never changes with the seasons.15 Many programs store every moment in UTC and convert to local time only when a person reads it.6

    When it went wrong

    In October 2010, Australians and New Zealanders woke to a strange bug. After the clocks went forward, repeating iPhone alarms went off an hour early.910 Apple's advice was to switch off the repeat and set the alarm again each day.9 For once, the clock change gave people an extra hour of being awake.

    That was not the first time. In 2007, the United States moved its daylight saving dates. Many computer systems had to be updated, and email and calendar programs were hit hardest.6

    Servers have their own version. Older versions of the Unix scheduler cron could run a job twice when the clocks went back.11 Modern versions skip the repeat, and run jobs from a skipped hour just after the change.12

    The volunteers who keep time

    So how does your phone know when Sydney changes its clocks? Almost every phone and computer gets its rules from one source, the tz database.7 It is also called the Olson database, after its founder, Arthur David Olson.7 Volunteers maintain it, led today by Paul Eggert and Tim Parenti.7 Changes are argued over on a public mailing list.7

    Governments do not always give them much warning. Western Australia's Parliament approved its daylight saving trial on 21 November 2006.8 The trial started on 3 December, 12 days later.8 A volunteer posted the news to the list the next day, adding: "The lack of notice is quite unfortunate".8 Microsoft rushed out updates for "the DST transition at short notice".14

    Morocco cut it even finer. On 26 October 2018, its government decided to stay on summer time for good.16 The clocks had been due to go back two days later.16 Even that "permanent" time has an exception. Morocco still moves its clocks back an hour for Ramadan. In 2026, that ran from 15 February to 22 March.16

    Why a vote helps here too

    A vote at each state election has a quiet bonus. Everyone would know the answer months ahead. NSW, for example, votes on 13 March 2027.18 A result that night could take effect from October, nearly seven months later. That is plenty of time for the volunteers, the phone makers and the people who book meeting rooms.

    Changing the rules is not the problem for software. Changing them by surprise is.

    The southern twist

    Australia changes its clocks in spring, when the US and Europe are changing theirs for autumn. The dates do not line up either.145

    Sydney moved forward on 4 October 2026. London goes back on 25 October, and New York on 1 November.145 So the gap to London went from 9 hours to 10, and will reach 11. The gap to New York went from 14 to 15, and will reach 16.17

    If you run a weekly call with London, it moves twice this October alone. Every invite between those cities needs a time zone attached, not just a time.

    Several governments are trying to stop the clocks changing for good. So where does each one stand? And where does that leave us?

    Australia: one MP's month

    There is no national rule here. Each state and territory sets its own time.2 This summer, NSW, Victoria, South Australia, Tasmania and the ACT use daylight saving.3 It began on Sunday 4 October 2026 and ends on Sunday 4 April 2027.1

    Geoff Provest, the member for Tweed, wants less of it. He introduced a bill on 28 May 2026.4 It would have run daylight saving from the first Sunday in November to the first Sunday in March, a month shorter at each end.5 It lapsed on 18 September 2026 without a vote.4

    It was not his first try. In 2016, he backed a colleague's push to cut a month. He said the last month is "a pain and drags on too long".18 Whatever you think of his idea, notice who would have decided. Ninety-three members of the Legislative Assembly, not the eight million people of NSW.19

    The United States: so close, again

    On 14 July 2026, the House of Representatives voted 308 to 117 to make daylight saving permanent.68 President Donald Trump has urged the Senate to pass it.9 The Senate needs 60 votes, and Majority Leader John Thune has doubted it can get them.9 Senator Tom Cotton leads the opposition, warning of dark winter mornings for children waiting for school buses.9

    Then the Senate adjourned for the midterm elections.9 American clocks go back on Sunday 1 November. The Senate returns on 9 November.9 You could not script it better.

    Europe: stuck since 2019

    In 2018, the European Commission asked the public. Of about 4.6 million replies, 84 per cent wanted the clock changes to end.12 The European Parliament voted for that in March 2019.11 National governments have never agreed on what to keep instead.1213 So Europe's clocks go back on Sunday 25 October 2026, as usual.13

    Europe at least asked. It just has not acted on the answer.

    Countries that stopped

    Some places have made the choice. Mexico ended daylight saving on 30 October 2022. Towns near the US border kept it, to stay in step with their neighbours.15 Brazil ended it by decree on 25 April 2019.16

    What I am asking for

    I am not asking for daylight saving to stay. I am not asking for it to go. I am asking for a say.

    Here is the idea. At each state election, add one question to the ballot paper: daylight saving for the next term, yes or no? Majority wins, and the answer holds until the next election. NSW did exactly this once, in 1976.20

    The next chances are close. Victoria votes on 28 November 2026.21 NSW votes on 13 March 2027.19

    If you have felt off since October

    Have you felt tired, flat or a little foggy since 4 October? The clock change may be part of the reason.

    On the first night, people in a UK study slept 65 minutes less.22 In a European study, night owls never fully adjusted to the spring change, even eight weeks later.23 The Sleep Health Foundation warns that the spring change "can increase the risk of sleep loss and drowsiness".3

    So ask the people you live with how they have been sleeping. You may find you are not the only one. And if tiredness lasts for weeks, it is worth seeing a GP. A clock is not the only possible cause.

    How to have your say

    1. Sign the petition for a vote. I started it alongside this post. It asks every Premier and Chief Minister to put one question on daylight saving at each state and territory election, with the majority deciding. It takes no side. Sign it here.
    2. Write to your state MP. Daylight saving is state law, so your state MP can act on it.2 Your federal MP cannot. Your state parliament's website lists members by electorate. A short email is enough. There is one below that you can copy.
    3. Sign a parliament petition too, if there is one. Parliament does not count petitions on change.org formally. It counts its own. In NSW, an online petition with 500 signatures needs a government response. One with 20,000 is debated in Parliament.24
    4. Ask your candidates. If you are in Victoria or NSW, an election is months away. Ask each candidate whether they would support a vote on daylight saving.
    5. Tell your family and friends. Send them this post, or just the short version. Tell them how the change made you feel. Ask how it made them feel.

    Getting through the next change

    Until then, the clocks keep moving. The next change here is the easy one. On 4 April 2027, we get the hour back.1

    The American Academy of Sleep Medicine has advice for that change. Move bedtime 15 to 20 minutes later each night for a few nights.17 If you already sleep too little, do not push your bedtime later.17

    October will come round again. For that one, the Sleep Health Foundation suggests going to bed 15 to 20 minutes earlier for three or four nights beforehand.3 Get bright light soon after you wake, to help your body clock catch up.3

    Sending that message takes less time than changing the clock on the oven. And unlike the oven, you only have to do it once.
